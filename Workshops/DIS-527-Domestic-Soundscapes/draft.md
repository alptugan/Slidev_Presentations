The Classification of Sounds in Acoustic Ecology: An Examination of Sound Signals, Soundmarks, and Emerging Categories1. Introduction to Acoustic Ecology and Soundscape Studies:Acoustic ecology, a field of study also known as ecoacoustics or soundscape studies, delves into the intricate relationships between living beings and their environment as mediated through sound. This inherently interdisciplinary domain draws upon a diverse range of expertise, including acoustics, psychoacoustics, musicology, biology, ethnomusicology, geography, sociology, and even urban planning. The initial explorations within acoustic ecology were largely centered on human perception of the sonic environment, with pioneers such as R. Murray Schafer and the World Soundscape Project (WSP) playing a pivotal role in shaping the field. Schafer's seminal work, "The Tuning of the World" (1977), laid a foundational framework for understanding our sonic surroundings.More recent advancements, particularly in the areas of soundscape ecology and ecoacoustics, have broadened the scope of inquiry. These contemporary approaches emphasize the ecological significance of all sounds within a landscape, encompassing the biological sounds produced by living organisms (biophony), the geophysical sounds originating from natural physical processes (geophony), and the human-produced sounds (anthrophony). This evolution signifies a transition from a primarily anthropocentric perspective, focused on human auditory experience and the impact of noise pollution , towards a more holistic ecological understanding that acknowledges the crucial role of sound in ecosystem dynamics and the maintenance of biodiversity. The development of new technologies for recording and analyzing soundscapes has been instrumental in facilitating this broader understanding of the sound environment and the ecological roles played by various sound sources. Sounds are not merely perceived by humans; they serve vital functions in animal communication, habitat selection, and the overall health of ecosystems.The inherently interdisciplinary nature of acoustic ecology underscores the intricate interplay between the physical properties of sound, the perceptual mechanisms of both humans and animals, and the ecological and cultural contexts in which sounds manifest. A comprehensive understanding of soundscapes necessitates knowledge from diverse fields. Acoustics provides the fundamental understanding of sound waves and their propagation, while psychoacoustics and biology elucidate how these sounds are processed and interpreted by living organisms. Ethnomusicology and sociology contribute valuable insights into the cultural and societal meanings attributed to different sounds. Ecology provides the overarching framework for comprehending the complex interactions between sounds and the environment. This integrated, multidisciplinary approach is essential for achieving a holistic understanding of the acoustic environments that shape our world.2. Traditional Soundscape Categories: Sound Signals:Within the framework of acoustic ecology, particularly in the context of human perception, sound signals occupy a distinct category. R. Murray Schafer defined sound signals as foreground sounds that are consciously attended to and are intended to capture attention. These signals often serve the purpose of conveying specific information, providing warnings, or issuing alerts. In contrast to the continuous or frequently occurring background sounds known as keynote sounds, sound signals stand out, acting as the prominent "figure" against the less noticeable "ground" of the soundscape. The very definition of sound signals emphasizes their role in communication and their ability to structure our conscious auditory experience within the broader sonic environment.Sound signals possess specific characteristics and fulfill important functions within an acoustic ecology. They are typically more distinct or exhibit a higher amplitude than the ambient keynote sounds, making them readily discernible. Furthermore, sound signals can be organized into intricate codes, enabling the transmission of complex messages to those who understand the system. Examples of such coded signals include train whistles that communicate specific maneuvers or Morse code used for long-distance communication. Beyond conveying explicit information, sound signals also play a crucial role in regulating the activities of a community and reflecting its unique character. The potential for sound signals to evolve into sophisticated communication systems underscores the remarkable ways in which both natural and human-generated sounds can transmit meaning within an acoustic environment.The realm of sound signals encompasses a wide array of examples, both from the natural world and from human endeavors. Natural sound signals include the diverse vocalizations of animals that serve various communicative purposes, such as attracting mates, warning of danger, or establishing territory. Human-made sound signals are equally prevalent and include familiar sounds like train whistles, car horns, ambulance sirens, the ringing of bells, telephone ringtones, and various types of alarms. Interestingly, some sounds can function in multiple categories depending on their context and perception. For instance, the sound of church bells can act as a sound signal, calling a community to worship, but it can also serve as a soundmark, uniquely identifying a particular place through its specific sonic characteristics. This dual functionality highlights the context-dependent nature of sound categorization within the study of acoustic ecology. A sound's classification is not always absolute but can shift based on how it is perceived and the specific role it plays within a given community or environment.3. Traditional Soundscape Categories: Soundmarks:Another crucial category within the traditional framework of acoustic ecology is that of soundmarks. These are defined as community-specific sounds that are unique to a particular location and possess qualities that make them especially valued or noticeable by the inhabitants of that area. The term "soundmark" is intentionally derived from "landmark," emphasizing the parallel role these sounds play in acoustically identifying a place. Soundmarks often hold significant cultural and historical value for a community. These auditory anchors contribute significantly to a community's sense of place and its collective identity, functioning much like visual landmarks in orienting and grounding individuals within their environment.The significance of soundmarks extends deeply into the realms of cultural heritage, community identity, and the development of a strong sense of place. Soundmarks can serve as powerful expressions of a community's unique identity, much like its distinct architectural styles, cherished customs, and traditional attire. They contribute significantly to the cultural heritage of a community and are therefore deserving of both preservation and active protection. Indeed, soundscapes, defined in large part by their characteristic soundmarks, can be instrumental in recognizing and distinguishing different settlements and communities. Even the act of remembering soundmarks from the past can offer valuable insights into the historical evolution of a community and its sonic environment. The importance of soundmarks transcends mere acoustic identification; they are deeply interwoven into the cultural fabric of a community, reflecting its history, its enduring traditions, and its shared collective memory.The diversity of sounds that can function as soundmarks is considerable, ranging from natural phenomena to human-created sounds that have become deeply associated with specific activities or traditions. Natural examples of soundmarks include the distinctive sounds of geysers erupting in a particular region or the unique roar of a specific waterfall. Cultural soundmarks encompass a wide range of human-generated sounds, such as the characteristic melodies of church bells in a town, the distinct sound of foghorns along a particular coastline, or the sounds associated with traditional crafts or festivals. In some instances, a soundmark can even reflect a nation's identity, as seen with the multilingual announcements in Singapore's public train stations. The sounds of church bells, due to their historical and cultural significance, are frequently regarded as important soundmarks with considerable heritage value. Recognizing the broad spectrum of sounds that can serve as soundmarks underscores the necessity for a comprehensive and inclusive approach to soundscape preservation. This involves not only safeguarding natural sounds but also acknowledging the cultural importance of human-created sounds that have become integral to a community's acoustic identity.4. Emerging Categories in Acoustic Ecology: A Review of Recent Literature:Recent academic publications in acoustic ecology reveal a field that continues to expand and evolve, moving beyond the traditional categories of sound signals and soundmarks. This evolution is driven by advancements in technology, such as sophisticated recording devices and analytical software, coupled with a deepening understanding of complex ecological processes. There has been a notable surge in research within soundscape ecology and ecoacoustics, with a strong emphasis on exploring the ecological characteristics and implications of sounds within various environments. Furthermore, a growing body of literature focuses on the practical application of acoustic ecology in conservation and ecological restoration efforts, highlighting the potential of sound-based approaches to address environmental challenges. The recent literature clearly indicates a dynamic progression within acoustic ecology, with the emergence of novel categories and conceptual frameworks designed to better understand the multifaceted nature of both natural and human-influenced acoustic environments.One significant emerging category is acoustic restoration. This approach focuses on actively utilizing soundscapes as a tool for ecological restoration. The core concept involves broadcasting carefully selected soundscapes in degraded or disturbed areas with the aim of attracting animals and accelerating the process of recolonization. This novel field is championed by researchers like Elizabeth Znidersic and David M Watson. The significance of acoustic restoration lies in its recognition of the intrinsic ecological and social values inherent in soundscapes and their potential to play a direct role in the recovery of damaged ecosystems. This represents a forward-thinking application of acoustic ecology principles, underscoring the active role that sound can play in ecological management and conservation. By understanding the crucial role of sound in animal behavior, including habitat selection and communication , researchers are investigating the use of targeted soundscape broadcasts to encourage the return of specific species to areas where they have declined or disappeared. This approach moves beyond simply mitigating negative sounds to proactively employing positive acoustic cues for ecological benefit.Another important development in the categorization of soundscapes is the framework proposed by Grinfeder et al. (2022), which introduces the concepts of distal, proximal, and perceptual soundscapes. This framework aims to provide greater clarity and operational utility to the definition of "soundscape," particularly within the context of ecological conservation. The distal soundscape is defined as the spatial and temporal distribution of sounds within a pre-defined area, taking into account the effects of sound propagation. This category represents the soundscape at a broad ecological scale, focusing on the sources of sound and their relationship with various environmental factors. The proximal soundscape, in contrast, refers to the collection of propagated sound signals that occur at a specific point in space. This level represents the filtered sounds as they reach a potential receiver, such as an animal or a recording device. Finally, the perceptual soundscape is defined as the individual, subjective interpretation of a proximal soundscape by a listener. This category emphasizes the subjective experience and cognitive processing of the acoustic environment. The proponents of this framework, including Elie Grinfeder, Christian Lorenzi, Sylvain Haupert, and Jérôme Sueur , argue that it offers a more operational approach to understanding soundscapes in ecological contexts, moving beyond a singular, all-encompassing definition. The introduction of these three distinct levels of soundscape provides a valuable refinement of the concept, acknowledging the different scales and perspectives involved in studying acoustic environments, from broad ecological patterns to the specific sounds experienced and interpreted by an individual.Beyond these explicitly proposed new categories, recent literature also highlights other evolving frameworks and analytical tools within acoustic ecology. The increasing utilization of acoustic indices, such as the Acoustic Complexity Index (ACI) and the Normalized Difference Soundscape Index (NDSI), offers novel ways to categorize and analyze soundscapes based on their statistical properties and their relationships to biodiversity and the impact of human activities. While not new categories of sound themselves, these indices provide quantitative methods for characterizing and comparing acoustic environments. Furthermore, the field of biotremology, which focuses on the study of substrate-borne vibrations, is being increasingly integrated into ecoacoustic research, expanding the scope of inquiry beyond traditional airborne sounds. This integration has the potential to lead to new categories related to vibrational signals and their ecological significance. Additionally, Waddington (2022) proposes a set of eight key types of soundscapes deemed important for conservation based on their ecological and social value: natural quiet soundscapes, sensitive soundscapes, threatened soundscapes, unique soundscapes, recreational soundscapes, representative soundscapes, cultural soundscapes, and everyday soundscapes. This represents a categorization framework specifically tailored to conservation priorities. These developments collectively indicate a trend towards more quantitative and application-oriented approaches to categorizing and understanding the role of sound within acoustic ecology.5. Comparison and Contrast of Sound Signals, Soundmarks, and New Categories:When comparing and contrasting the traditional soundscape categories of sound signals and soundmarks, several key distinctions and similarities emerge. Both sound signals and soundmarks are considered foreground sounds that are consciously perceived by listeners, setting them apart from the often-unnoticed background of keynote sounds. However, their primary functions differ significantly. Sound signals primarily serve to convey specific information, issue warnings, or act as alerts within an environment. In contrast, soundmarks are unique sounds that are strongly associated with a particular place and often contribute significantly to the identity and sense of place for the community residing there. Soundmarks frequently carry cultural and historical significance for a community, playing a vital role in fostering a sense of place and collective memory , whereas sound signals are generally more focused on immediate communication or conveying timely alerts. Despite these functional differences, there can be instances of overlap, where a single sound, such as the ringing of church bells, can function both as a sound signal (e.g., calling people to worship) and as a soundmark, uniquely identifying a specific church or community through its distinct sonic characteristics. This potential for overlap underscores the inherent complexity in categorizing sounds within acoustic ecology.The newly proposed categories in acoustic ecology relate to the traditional classifications in various ways. Acoustic restoration represents an application-oriented approach that may utilize sound signals, such as species-specific vocalizations, as a means to achieve ecological conservation goals. It does not replace the traditional categories but rather leverages them strategically. The framework of distal, proximal, and perceptual soundscapes offers a broader perspective on understanding the soundscape as a whole. Within this framework, sounds that might be classified as signals or soundmarks in Schafer's system would likely be considered as specific components contributing to the proximal and perceptual levels of the soundscape. Acoustic indices provide quantitative tools for analyzing the overall soundscape, including the presence and characteristics of potential sound signals and the acoustic diversity that might indicate the presence of unique soundmarks. While they offer a valuable means of describing and comparing soundscapes, they do not replace the qualitative categorization of individual sounds. Similarly, soundscape typologies for conservation categorize entire soundscapes based on their overall characteristics and conservation value, which could include the presence and quality of specific soundmarks and the balance of biophony, geophony, and anthrophony. Sound signals might serve as important indicators within certain soundscape types identified for conservation. In general, these newly proposed categories and frameworks tend to complement rather than supersede the traditional categories of sound signals and soundmarks, providing broader and more nuanced contexts for their understanding and application.Academic literature often discusses Schafer's tripartite classification of keynote sounds, sound signals, and soundmarks as a foundational framework for comprehending soundscapes. The functional description of "soundscape" provided by Grinfeder et al. (2022) implicitly encompasses all types of sounds within their multi-layered framework of distal, proximal, and perceptual soundscapes. Furthermore, Engel et al. (2022) conducted a classification of perceived sound sources in an urban environment, including keynote sounds, sound signals, and soundmarks, based on a range of perceptual, socio-cultural, and objective parameters. While direct comparative analyses between Schafer's sound categories and the newer frameworks might be somewhat limited in the existing literature, sound signals and soundmarks are frequently contextualized within broader discussions of soundscapes and their ecological or perceptual dimensions. This suggests an evolving understanding within the field, where traditional categorizations are being integrated into more comprehensive and multi-layered models of acoustic environments.6. Conclusion: The Evolving Landscape of Sound in Acoustic Ecology:The exploration of academic literature on acoustic ecology reveals a dynamic and evolving field. Sound signals, as foreground sounds conveying information, and soundmarks, as unique sounds defining place and identity, remain fundamental concepts in understanding our sonic environment. Recent academic work has proposed new categories that broaden the scope of acoustic ecology. Acoustic restoration offers a novel application of soundscapes for ecological recovery, while the framework of distal, proximal, and perceptual soundscapes provides a more nuanced and multi-layered approach to analyzing acoustic environments at different scales. These new frameworks complement rather than replace the traditional categories, offering broader contexts for their interpretation and application.The field of acoustic ecology is characterized by ongoing efforts to refine the categorization of sounds and soundscapes to better understand the complex interactions within our sonic world. Future research could fruitfully explore the specific relationships between the traditional categories of sound signals and soundmarks and these newly proposed frameworks. Investigating the applicability and utility of these categories across diverse ecological and cultural contexts would further enhance our understanding. A significant challenge remains in developing standardized methodologies for identifying and analyzing different sound categories across various research areas within acoustic ecology.
